How it works

What Dijkstra actually does

Dijkstra never guesses. It settles the closest unvisited intersection first, every time, so it always finds the true shortest route. It can't tell which streets don't matter until it's already reached them — press play and watch the orange dots spread out in every direction: perfect, and blind.

What to notice: the flood reaches streets nowhere near the winning route — Dijkstra can't skip them without already knowing the answer.

The hierarchy, revealed

Every contraction order ranks every intersection — and on a real road network, that ranking isn't random. The busiest roads — bridges, parkways, the ones everything else feeds into — rank highest, because removing them early would need shortcuts everywhere. The tour below plays itself, or drag the slider and take the wheel any time.

What to notice: the lake bridges survive to the top — bottleneck nodes rank high.

The query: only ever climb

Once every intersection has a rank, finding the shortest route is simple: climb up from the start, climb up from the end, and stop where they meet. This never misses a route — every shortest path has one highest point on it, and shortcuts guarantee both climbs can reach that summit without ever stepping down. Press play and watch it happen twice at once: the climb above, the same route unzipping street by street on the real map below.

What to notice: the climbs only ever move up in rank, never down — they barely touch the map below, and the winning route rides the big road.

Shortcuts: the price of forgetting

Chapter 2's ranking is really a demolition schedule: CH removes intersections one at a time, quietest first — under one rule, nothing is allowed to get slower. Before an intersection goes, every way through it gets the same question: is there already a detour just as fast without it? If yes, it goes for free — that detour is called a witness. If no, CH paves a bypass first: a shortcut that carries exactly the travel time the trip through used to take. Click any intersection below and watch it ask, pair by pair.

What to notice: the same question, asked once per pair of roads — a detour just as fast means a free pass; no detour means a dashed shortcut, priced at exactly the old travel time.

Order is everything

Every shortcut is clutter the map has to carry, so the demolition order decides everything. Press the buttons below and watch each order play out on the map: knock out a busy hub early and shortcuts bury the streets within seconds; save the hubs for last and the map stays almost clean. That's the whole trick — contract quiet streets first, and the survivors are exactly the big roads. It's the hierarchy you saw revealed in chapter 2, and it's why the climb in chapter 3 works. Then take "your turn" and see if you can beat the heuristic.

What to notice: the smart order isn't luck — run after run it adds the fewest shortcuts, and the last streets standing are the ones you'd call main roads.
